1. Introduction¶

Formula 1 is often described as a data-driven sport, where milliseconds determine the difference between victory and defeat. While the drivers are the public face of the sport, success is a complex function of engineering reliability, race strategy, pit stops, qualifying performance, and driver skill.

This project analyzes historical data from the Formula 1 World Championship (1950-2025) to quantify these factors. By merging race results, telemetry, and constructor data, this report aims to answer the fundamental question: "What actually leads to success in modern F1?"

Questions to be Answered:¶

  1. Who are the most statistically dominant drivers in history?
  2. How has the speed of pit stops evolved?
  3. How critical is Qualifying?
  4. Which top constructors in the Hybrid Era (2014-Present) have suffered the worst mechanical reliability?
  5. Does the "Home Field Advantage" exist in F1?
  6. What countries are the most successful in Formula 1?

These questions are interesting to me as I am a big Formula 1 fan and have been following the sport for a long time. I am curious to see how much of an impact each factor has on statistical success.

2. Data Files¶

These data files are all obtained from a dataset hosted on Kaggle.com that originally came from the F1 API:

https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/rohanrao/formula-1-world-championship-1950-2020/data

The dataset contains several files that are used in this analysis:

  • drivers.csv: Drivers' personal information and statistics.
  • results.csv: Race results and qualifying performance.
  • circuits.csv: Circuit information and geographic location.
  • constructors.csv: Constructor information and statistics.
  • status.csv: Status messages for each race result.
  • pit_stops.csv: Pit stop information and telemetry.
  • races.csv: Race information and results.
  • driver_standings.csv: Driver standings at the end of each race.

4.4: Which top constructor has the worst reliability in the Hybrid Era?¶

We analyzed the "Hybrid Era" (2014–Present) to see which teams suffered the most mechanical failures. We merged results with status and constructors. We filter for status messages containing "Engine," "Gearbox", or "Clutch", excluding accidents (which are driver error) to place the importance of a team's reliability. A simple bar graph will show us which teams had the worst reliability.

In [26]:
# --- Question 4: Manufacturer Reliability ---

# Load the necessary files
results_df = pd.read_csv('data/results.csv')
races_df = pd.read_csv('data/races.csv')
status_df = pd.read_csv('data/status.csv')
constructors_df = pd.read_csv('data/constructors.csv')

# Merge 1: Results + Races (to get the Year)
df = pd.merge(results_df, races_df[['raceId', 'year']], on='raceId', how='left')

# Merge 2: + Status (to know why they stopped)
df = pd.merge(df, status_df, on='statusId', how='left')

# Merge 3: + Constructors (to get the Team Name)
df = pd.merge(df, constructors_df[['constructorId', 'name']], on='constructorId', how='left')

# Filter for Hybrid Era (2014 onwards)
hybrid_era = df[df['year'] >= 2014]

# Define "Mechanical Failure"
# We exclude accidents and "Finished" statuses.
# We look for keywords that indicate the CAR broke.
mechanical_keywords = [
    'Engine', 'Gearbox', 'Transmission', 'Clutch', 'Hydraulics',
    'Electrical', 'Power Unit', 'Brakes', 'Suspension', 'Turbo',
    'Exhaust', 'Overheating', 'Mechanical', 'Battery', 'Radiator',
    'Driveshaft', 'Hydraulics', 'Differential', 'Fuel Pressure',
    'Water Pressure', 'Throttle', 'Steering', 'Technical', 'Heat shield fire',
    'Water leak', 'Fuel pump', 'Track rod', 'Oil pressure', 'Pneumatics',
    'Wheel bearing', 'Fuel system', 'Drivetrain', 'Power loss', 'Fuel',
    'Ignition', 'Battery', 'Chassis', 'Crankshaft', 'Alternator', 'Oil pump',
    'Injection', 'Spark plugs', 'Fuel pipe', 'Oil pipe', 'Water pipe',
    'Power unit', 'ERS', 'Cooling system'
]

# Create a filter pattern (e.g., "Engine|Gearbox|Clutch")
pattern = '|'.join(mechanical_keywords)

# Filter for rows where the status string contains one of our keywords
mechanical_failures = hybrid_era[hybrid_era['status'].str.contains(pattern, case=False, na=False)]

# Analyze: Count failures by Team
failure_counts = mechanical_failures['name'].value_counts().head(10)

# Visualize
plt.figure(figsize=(12, 6))
sns.barplot(
    x=failure_counts.values,
    y=failure_counts.index,
    hue=failure_counts.index,
    palette='Reds', # Red palette fits the "danger/failure" theme
    legend=False
)

# Show
plt.title('Mechanical Failures by Team (Hybrid Era: 2014-Present)')
plt.xlabel('Number of DNFs due to Car Failure')
plt.show()

Observation:

The data highlights the struggle of manufacturers like McLaren (during the troubled Honda partnership) and Renault. In contrast, Mercedes shows a lower frequency of mechanical failures, suggesting their dominance in this era was built much on bulletproof reliability. Note that the Renault F1 team became the Alpine F1 team in 2021, so the Renault operation as a whole can be considered to have very poor reliability. Including stats from their customer teams such as Red-bull and McLaren, although those teams have since moved on to different engine partners.

4.5: Does "Home Field Advantage" exist in F1?¶

Unlike other sports, F1 drivers do not train on their home track daily. However, the psychological boost of a home crowd is often discussed. We performed a 4-way merge (Drivers -> Results -> Races -> Circuits). We mapped demonyms (e.g., 'Australian') to countries (e.g., 'Australia') of the main F1 driver + track combinations and compared average finishing positions. We can use a bar graph to visualize the results.

5. Conclusions and Discussions¶

The primary objective of this project was to deconstruct the factors contributing to success in Formula 1. By analyzing over 70 years of race data, we have determined that victory is rarely a result of chance, but rather a predictable outcome of driver skill and team performance working in harmony. With an emphasis on constant optimization of both these factors, a Formula 1 team can achieve statistical success.

The analysis of qualifying data (Q3) revealed a strong correlation between starting grid position and victory probability. This correlation is not surprising, as the more a driver is ahead of the pack, the more likely they are to win. While this does signify the importance of having a skilled driver on the team, it also highlights the importance of team dominance. Pole position in qualifying has a 43% win conversion rate. Any constructor would have to work on giving its drivers a mechanically superior car to give them a higher chance of qualifying 1st. This is further illustrated by the dominance of specific drivers (Q1), whose high win counts correlate with cars that were statistically dominant in one-lap pace.

Formula 1 is largely seen as the pinnacle of motorsport, with a very high level of engineering expertise. Teams are constantly improving performance and optimizing strategies to gain even just milliseconds on their rivals. This is reflected in Q4 where teams with high reliability in the Hybrid Era (Such as Mercedes) have a much higher win rate than teams with poor reliability. This is also illustrated in Q2 where the number of pit-stops taken during a race distance has decreased significantly over time as cars become more mechanically robust. Even after rule changes, teams will find loop-holes and ways to optimize their performance.

Finally, the analysis of the home field advantage (Q5) revealed that the sport is not always dominated by pure numbers and engineering. The results of Q5 show that while small, there is a quantitative difference between driver's performance at home and away from home crowds. While this could be considered negligible, it could also suggest that the added pressures of racing in front of a home crowd could have negative psychological consequences for a driver, indicating there is always a human element to racing. This could also be inferred from the fact that the most successful drivers come from countries with Formula 1 ingrained into their culture such as the UK and Germany (Q6).
